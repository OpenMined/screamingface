# Bruce Schneier
**Role:** Fellow and Lecturer, Harvard Kennedy School Belfer Center; Board Member, Electronic Frontier Foundation; Chief of Security Architecture, Inrupt
**Category:** ABC Citations — P2

## Who They Are
Bruce Schneier is the most widely read public intellectual working at the intersection of security, privacy, and power. Author of more than a dozen books — including *Data and Goliath* (2015), *Click Here to Kill Everybody* (2018), and *A Hacker's Mind* (2023) — he has spent three decades translating technical security concepts into political and institutional arguments. His central thesis has evolved from cryptographic design toward a systemic critique: that concentrated digital power, not any individual vulnerability, is the defining security problem of our era. He writes a widely-read newsletter (*Crypto-Gram*), blogs at schneier.com, and testifies regularly before Congress.

## Relationship to AI Tools
Primarily a critic and analyst rather than a builder or heavy user. Schneier has written extensively about AI as a security surface and as a power concentration vector — he's skeptical of hype but rigorous about threat modeling. He does use AI tools in his writing and research workflow, but his public posture is that of someone who understands the internals well enough to distrust the incentives of the companies building them.

## Likely Reaction to screamingface
Schneier would be drawn to screamingface's structural argument before its technical one. The ensemble architecture directly addresses his long-standing concern that single-vendor AI creates dangerous chokepoints — both for privacy (one company sees all your queries) and for power (one company can cut off access). He'd likely write about it as a security architecture story: diversity of AI providers as resilience against both surveillance and supply disruption. He'd be characteristically cautious about whether open-source distribution actually neutralizes the power concentration problem if the underlying models are still controlled by a handful of companies. Expect a blog post that praises the principle while interrogating the limits.

## Key Tension
If the models themselves remain proprietary and centrally controlled, does routing prompts through an open ensemble meaningfully distribute power — or does it just add a decentralized UI layer on top of the same concentrated infrastructure?
