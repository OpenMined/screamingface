# Carissa Véliz
**Role:** Associate Professor of Philosophy, Oxford Institute for Ethics in AI; Fellow, Hertford College, University of Oxford
**Category:** ABC Citations — P4

## Who They Are
Carissa Véliz is one of the most prominent philosophers working on privacy as a political and social value, best known for *Privacy Is Power* (Penguin, 2020), which was named an Economist Book of the Year and has been translated into more than a dozen languages. Her argument is not primarily technical but moral and structural: that the erosion of privacy is a form of political domination, and that data markets systematically transfer power from individuals and communities to corporations and states. At Oxford's Institute for Ethics in AI, she works on the ethical foundations of AI development, including questions of consent, surveillance, and the governance of AI systems. She is a regular commentator in the international press and has advised the European Commission, UK Parliament, and US Congress on data and AI regulation.

## Relationship to AI Tools
A philosophical critic and policy voice rather than a practitioner. Véliz does not position herself as anti-technology but as pro-accountability — she is skeptical of AI systems that obscure how they work, who benefits, and who bears the risks. Her engagement with AI tools is shaped by her research: she cares deeply about what data AI systems consume, what they expose, and who controls the information flows.

## Likely Reaction to screamingface
Véliz would be genuinely interested but would arrive at screamingface through the data sovereignty lens rather than the performance or cost lens. The privacy-preserving ensemble architecture — where no single provider accumulates a complete record of a user's coding work — aligns with her core argument that structural protections matter more than individual consent mechanisms. She'd likely be drawn to the credit-sharing feature as a potential model for AI access as a commons rather than a commodity. Her concern would center on what data the ensemble itself collects: if screamingface logs queries for routing purposes, she'd want to know exactly what's stored, where, and for how long. She's a credible voice to co-author a piece on open AI infrastructure as a privacy architecture.

## Key Tension
Is screamingface's privacy protection genuine — built into the architecture — or is it merely an improvement over single-provider surveillance while still accumulating data in ways that reproduce the power asymmetries Véliz argues we need to dismantle?
