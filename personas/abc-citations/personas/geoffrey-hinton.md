# Geoffrey Hinton
**Role:** Professor Emeritus, University of Toronto; Nobel Laureate in Physics (2024); Turing Award recipient (2018)
**Category:** ABC Citations — P16

## Who They Are
Geoffrey Hinton is widely regarded as the "godfather of deep learning" — his foundational work on backpropagation, Boltzmann machines, and deep neural networks underpins virtually every modern AI system. His co-authored papers on deep learning (LeCun, Bengio, Hinton 2015) and ImageNet (Krizhevsky, Sutskever, Hinton 2012) are among the most cited in the field and are referenced directly in the ABC thesis. He left Google in May 2023 explicitly to speak without institutional constraint about AI existential risk, and has since become the most prominent scientific voice arguing that advanced AI systems pose a civilizational threat — particularly when concentrated in the hands of a few companies competing under market pressure to move fast and cut safety corners.

## Relationship to AI Tools
Hinton is the architect behind the tools, not a user of them. He built the intellectual machinery that Claude, Gemini, and every other frontier model runs on. His current relationship to AI tools is that of a deeply worried creator: he believes the systems descended from his research may become uncontrollable, and that the competitive dynamics driving their development — speed over safety, scale over alignment — are structurally dangerous. He engages as a public intellectual and advisor, not as a hands-on practitioner.

## Likely Reaction to screamingface
Hinton would find the decentralization thesis compelling but insufficient. The idea that no single company should control the AI inference pipeline aligns directly with his warnings about concentration risk — he has said repeatedly that the competitive race between Google, OpenAI, and a handful of others is one of the most dangerous dynamics in AI development. An open ensemble that routes across multiple models and providers structurally reduces single-point-of-failure risk, which he would acknowledge. But his deeper concern is about the capabilities themselves, not just who controls them. He would ask whether screamingface, by making frontier AI more accessible and efficient, accelerates the timeline to systems that are genuinely difficult to control — regardless of how many providers are in the mix. The attribution-based control framework would interest him as a governance mechanism, but he would want to know whether it can constrain systems that are smarter than the humans auditing them.

## Key Tension
Does distributing AI capability across an open ensemble meaningfully reduce existential risk, or does it accelerate the proliferation of systems powerful enough to be dangerous — making the alignment problem harder, not easier, by putting frontier capability in more hands with less oversight?
